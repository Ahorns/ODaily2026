# ODaily 2026 — the design

This is the intent behind the site. `README.md` is how to run it; this is why it
is shaped the way it is.

## The problem

Daily logs die in month two. Not because writing them is hard, but because
nothing accumulates — a folder of dated files looks exactly as interesting on
day two hundred as it did on day three. The work is real and the record of it is
inert.

So the design question is not "how do I store a day". It is: **what makes a year
of days worth coming back to look at?**

## The idea

A year is a galaxy, and you fly through it.

| In the log | In the sky |
|:--|:--|
| A day | A planet |
| A month | A stellar system |
| The year | A galaxy |
| A project | A constellation, traced across whatever nights it appeared in |
| A group of projects | A constellation family |
| An idea recorded | A comet |
| A milestone | A ring around the world |
| A day with no entry | Asteroid debris — the gap is recorded, not hidden |
| About me | Earth |

The constellations are the important one. Days live on a time axis and projects
cut across it, and a constellation is exactly that: a pattern that ignores the
grid it is drawn on. It lets the site be browsed by *when* and by *what*
without needing two separate navigations.

## Two registers

The site is deliberately two things, and they must not blur into each other.

- **What you navigate** is the universe. It can be as ornamental as it likes.
- **What you read** is an entry. Quiet, narrow, text first, nothing moving.

Same palette in both, so it still reads as one place. The metaphor stops at the
door of an entry. A log you cannot comfortably read is a toy.

## Nothing is placed by hand

Every planet is derived from its own day's data, so the same entry always
produces the same world and the map cannot drift out of step with the record.

| Planet property | Driven by |
|:--|:--|
| Terrain pattern | Hash of the date |
| Diameter | Hours worked |
| **Colour** | **The projects worked that day, blended by hours** |
| Terrain type | The dominant category that day |
| Moons | Extra projects beyond the first |
| Ring | A milestone |
| Comet | An idea was recorded |
| Name | A real star, exoplanet or moon, from the date |

Colour and terrain are separate on purpose: **colour says what you worked on,
terrain says how**. Two writing days on different projects look different; two
coding days on the same project look related.

Position is not decorative either: **the orbit is the day of the month**. The 1st
hugs the star and the 31st rides the rim, one body per orbit, which is what a
stellar system looks like and what a calendar dial does not. The angle carries
nothing, so it is scattered by the golden angle — the spacing a sunflower uses —
and the days never line up into spokes.

Putting the date in the radius alone is what lets the angles move: every day
keeps its own clock, falling off as r^-1.5 exactly as Kepler requires, so inner
worlds visibly overtake outer ones. Nothing about the record is lost by it.

## The arc: universe first, reading second

1. You arrive on the **whole** galaxy, with today's planet at the exact centre
   of the screen.
2. You **travel** — scroll and drag. No pagination, no leaving the page. While
   the universe is open it keeps the scroll wheel.
3. **Clicking** a planet loads that day into the journal below. It does not
   navigate away; you stay where you are.
4. There is a **deliberate exit**. Only then does the page hand the scroll back
   and drop you to the writing.

The one rule underneath all of that: exploring must never cost you your place.

## One visual language

The site is pixel art: everything is drawn into a small integer-sized buffer and
scaled up by a whole number, so every pixel on screen is a deliberate one. Lit
worlds in deep space — spheres of value noise quantised to a five-colour ramp,
over ordered-dithered nebulae.

An earlier version carried four styles at once, and then two. Carrying one means
every decision can be made for *this* look rather than for whatever survives
translation into three others.

## Every collection has a map and a list

The map is for browsing and thinking. The list is a dense sortable table for
finding what you did on 12 March. Small screens open on the list, because a
draggable universe on a phone is a demo, not a tool.

## The data, and who owns it

- `log/*.qmd` — one file per day, with a `sessions:` list carrying per-project
  hours and a category. Written by hand. Yours.
- `projects.yml` — the constellation registry, keyed by **permanent slugs**, so
  grouping and naming can be redesigned without touching a single past entry.
- Everything else — the sky data, the constellation pages, the totals, the
  summary blocks inside day files — is generated by `scripts/build.py` and is
  expected to be overwritten.

Sessions are a list rather than one project per file because a real day is often
three projects, and Quarto listings are file-granular.

## What success looks like

That the author still logs in month seven — and that opening the site in month
seven is more interesting than it was in month one, because there is more sky.
